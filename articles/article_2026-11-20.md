## ARTICLE

*When the bridge in *The Bridge on the River Kwai* was sabotaged, the war‑machine on both sides felt the tremor of a single point of failure. Today, the Strait of Hormuz plays a comparable role for global energy markets, and its recent turbulence is reshaping the strategic calculus of producers, consumers and investors alike.*

### **Why:** What is the exposure, and why does it matter now?

The Strait of Hormuz, a 21‑mile waterway linking the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman, carries roughly **20 % of the world’s petroleum liquids and about 30 % of its liquefied natural gas (LNG) cargoes** (IEA, 2026‑11‑20). The ongoing Iran‑U.S. confrontation has intermittently threatened to close the passage, prompting the IEA to warn that “the Hormuz crisis set to reshape global energy markets” (IEA, 2026‑11‑20).  

The immediate exposure is twofold. First, spot oil prices surged to multi‑year highs in early November 2026, prompting governments to intervene (Major Powers Vow…, 2026‑11‑19). Second, downstream sectors—refining, petrochemicals, fertilizer production—depend on uninterrupted feedstock deliveries; any sustained bottleneck forces rapid price adjustments and inventory drawdowns.  

Why now? The Iran war, which began in August 2026, has already produced the “largest supply disruption on record” according to the IEA (2026‑11‑19). Unlike previous regional skirmishes, the conflict coincides with a tight global oil market, heightened geopolitical rivalries and an accelerating energy transition that leaves little slack for prolonged supply shocks. The risk of a “single‑point‑failure” scenario is therefore immediate and material for every economy that imports oil or gas.

### **How:** What responses exist, and what are their concrete capacities and limits?

**1. Diversification of supply routes** – Nations are accelerating projects that bypass Hormuz. The United Arab Emirates has fast‑tracked the **Al Zour LNG terminal**, adding 7.5 Mtpa of regasification capacity (as of November 20 2026). Meanwhile, the **East‑West pipeline** in Saudi Arabia, slated for 2028, will link Gulf production directly to Red Sea ports, reducing reliance on the strait. However, these assets require years of construction, significant capital (estimated $15 bn for Al Zour), and face permitting bottlenecks.

**2. Strategic petroleum reserves (SPR) drawdown** – The United States, China and Russia have pledged coordinated increases in refined product output (Major Powers Vow…, 2026‑11‑19). In the short term, the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) reports that the U.S. SPR holds **714 million barrels**, enough to offset roughly two weeks of domestic consumption (EIA, 2026‑11‑19). While useful for price stabilization, SPR drawdowns are finite and can’t substitute for ongoing supply.

**3. Expansion of tanker fleets and alternate shipping lanes** – Shipbuilders such as **Hyundai Heavy Industries** and **Daewoo Shipbuilding** have accelerated orders for ultra‑large crude carriers (ULCCs) capable of longer voyages that avoid the strait. The **“Southern Route”** around the Cape of Good Hope, though longer, is gaining market share; in Q3 2026, 12 % of global crude shipments used this detour (IEA, 2026‑11‑20). The limitation is higher fuel consumption and increased transit time, which raises freight costs and emissions.

**4. Accelerated renewable and hydrogen investment** – Some oil‑importing nations are fast‑tracking renewable capacity to blunt future exposure. Saudi Arabia’s **NEOM Green Hydrogen project** aims for 4 GW of electrolyzer capacity by 2030, potentially offsetting 10 % of its domestic fuel demand (IEA, 2026‑11‑19). Yet the technology remains capital‑intensive and dependent on cheap electricity, limiting near‑term impact.

### **To what extent:** How far do these responses reduce the risk, and what are the timelines, costs, and open uncertainties?

Collectively, the measures can **moderate short‑term price spikes but will not eliminate structural vulnerability**. The SPR drawdown can cushion the market for **4‑6 weeks**, after which refineries must rely on alternative imports or domestic output (EIA, 2026‑11‑19). New infrastructure like Al Zour and the Saudi East‑West pipeline will only become operational **mid‑2028**, leaving a gap of roughly **18 months** before they meaningfully divert cargoes.  

The Southern Route’s share, while growing, adds an estimated **$5‑$7 per barrel** to freight costs (IEA, 2026‑11‑20), a burden that may be passed to end‑users, especially in emerging economies with limited fiscal space. Moreover, geopolitical uncertainties—such as potential sanctions on Iranian shipping or escalation of naval confrontations—could abruptly reverse any gains from route diversification.  

In the medium term, the **hydrogen and renewable push** offers a pathway to decouple from Hormuz altogether, but cost‑competitiveness is still uncertain. Current electrolyzer CAPEX averages **$1,200 kW⁻¹** (IEA, 2026‑11‑19); achieving scale‑economies that bring this below $800 kW⁻¹ may take a decade. Until then, oil and gas will remain the backbone of global energy, and the strait’s strategic importance will persist.

### **Shariah Screening Note for the Sectors**

1. **Pipeline & Subsea Engineering** – Generally permissible as the core activity is asset‑backed infrastructure. Screening issues include high interest‑bearing debt (many firms exceed AAOIFI’s 33 % debt‑to‑assets threshold) and potential revenue from non‑permissible activities (e.g., transport of alcohol‑related chemicals). Investors should verify debt structures and ensure revenue streams are primarily from permissible hydrocarbons.

2. **Strategic Storage & Terminal Operations** – Typically Shariah‑compliant because storage is a tangible asset. However, some operators earn fees from non‑energy cargoes (e.g., chemicals, petrochemicals) that may contain haram substances. Debt levels are often moderate, but the market‑cap denominator trap can cause a compliant company to fail screening during market downturns; ongoing monitoring is essential.
